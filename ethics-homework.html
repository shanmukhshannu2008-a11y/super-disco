Homework 3: AI Ethics Case Studies

Shanmukh Aravapalli · Two fictional cases: Auto Insurance and Essay Grader.

Case: Auto Insurance

SureDrive Insurance company uses an AI tool called the PriceRight to decide how much customers pay for their car insurance. It looks at ones driving records, information from a phone app, and other such details. A consumer group found out that people in certain zip codes pay more even when their driving records, accident histories, and cars are exactly the same. The tool does not directly use any of kind of race or income, but some details like where a person lives, their phone type, and when they drive can indirectly reflect these things. The company knows this but still tells customers the tool is fair. Some agents also secretly change information to give friends and known customers lower prices, and the management allows it as they are the best employees.

I think the main problem here is that the customers can be treated unfairly for something they have little to do with how safely they drive. For example, someone working a night shift won’t have a choice of when they drive. So its SureDrive's responsibility to check whether these details actually justify the higher prices. Such extra costs could make life harder for people already struggling to pay bills, especially if they need a car to get to work. It is also unfair that some customers get better prices simply because they know an agent. in my perspective, SureDrive should be honest about the tool’s problems, explain its pricing, and give customers a way to question their rates. Employees should also have to record and explain any changes they make as they cannot favour some and not with the others.

Case: Essay Grader

The Springfield School District uses an AI tool called ScoreWrite to grade students’ year-end essays. The district says the tool grades more consistently than actual people, and many teachers started relying on its reports instead of giving detailed feedback themselves. A researcher found out that the tool gave lower scores to students learning English and those who use language patterns from their own communities, even when their ideas and organization are just as strong and similar to others. This probably happened because the essays they used to train it mostly followed a single narrow academic writing style. The district also starts using these scores to evaluate teachers, even though the tool was never tested for that purpose or use case.

I think the biggest issue here is that ScoreWrite does not fairly measure what a student actually understood. Clear writing does matter, but a Student should not be receiving a lower score for a different language style when the exam is supposed to measure their reasoning and not all these other aspects. A tool can give consistent scores and still be consistently unfair to different students. Students could lose confidence or focus more on writing for the software than explaining their ideas in their own voice. With less feedback from teachers, they may also miss advice that would actually help them improve in these areas. in this case, teachers could also be judged unfairly if they teach more students whose writing is being graded poorly by the tool. The district should check the tool’s results across different groups, have teachers review wrong grades, and give them detailed feedback. It should also definitely stop using these scores to judge teachers without knowing if they can fairly measure teaching quality.
