LEARNING OUTCOME / 03

Describe how core AI techniques such as search and adversarial reasoning are used to model intelligent behavior.

FIRST DRAFT

How AI Works Through a Problem

When I use an AI tool, I usually focus on the answer it gives me. Learning about search made me pay more attention to how a system reaches an answer. It needs a way to describe the situation it is in, the actions it can take, and the goal it wants to reach. It can then compare what might happen after different actions. Those possibilities do not have to be physical places. They could be different course schedules, arrangements of objects, or positions in a game.

One thing that became clearer to me is that “best” depends on the problem. Breadth-First Search, or BFS, checks possibilities one level at a time. When every move has the same cost, it can find a solution with the fewest moves. In my course-scheduling homework, I used BFS to rearrange six classes while following prerequisite rules. Each move swapped two neighboring classes, and my solution took three swaps. Since I wanted the fewest changes and every swap counted equally, BFS made sense. Depth-First Search works differently. It follows one branch before backing up and trying another. It can find a solution, but that solution is not necessarily the shortest.

The cave lab showed me why the fewest moves might not be what we actually want. Our BFS route had five passages and used 14 minutes of light. The A* route had six passages but used only 12 minutes. Taking an extra passage saved light because the passages had different costs. A* considers both the cost so far and an estimate of what is left. That estimate, called a heuristic, helps guide the search. It is useful, but it needs to be chosen carefully. An estimate that is too high can lead A* away from the cheapest solution. This made me think about how an app could give a technically correct answer while focusing on the wrong goal for its user.

Another challenge is deciding what to do when someone else gets a turn. In a game, a move might look great until the opponent responds. Minimax works through those possible responses. One player tries to get the highest score, while the other tries to make that score as low as possible. In our Scout and Raven lab, Scout’s best starting move was West Colonnade, with a value of 3. The other starting moves had values of 1 after accounting for Raven’s responses. Scout was choosing a move that held up against a strong opponent, rather than relying on Raven to make a mistake.

Checking all those possibilities can take a lot of work. Alpha-beta pruning reduces that work by skipping branches that cannot change the decision. In our lab, plain minimax evaluated 28 nodes, while alpha-beta evaluated 18 and still recommended the same move. I found that interesting because doing less work did not mean accepting a worse answer. There was a reason those branches could be skipped. In bigger games, even pruning may not be enough, so a system can limit how far ahead it searches and estimate how good a position is. Those estimates are useful, but they are not the same as knowing how the game will end.

As someone who enjoys building apps, I can see these ideas being useful in a scheduling or planning feature. Before choosing an algorithm, I would need to decide what the user actually wants to improve. Is it fewer changes, less time, or lower cost? I would also need to think about whether other people’s decisions could affect the plan. What I took from this topic is that a useful AI system needs more than a way to search. It needs a clear goal and a description of the problem that includes what matters.

  • Week 4 Lab: The Last Charge. Team work with Shrey Patel, especially Questions 18–20 comparing BFS, DFS, and A*, their route costs, and the role of the heuristic.
  • Week 5 Lab: Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning, Traced By Hand. Team work with Josh Dcosta, especially Questions 6, 11, 12, and 15 on Scout’s best move and pruning branches without changing the result.
