ETHICS · ENTRY 01

Looking Beyond Whether AI Works

One thing I learned from this topic is that an AI system can apply the same rules to everyone and still treat people unfairly. In my homework’s fictional insurance case, the tool did not directly use race or income, but details like ZIP code could still reflect those differences. The essay-grading case showed another problem: students could receive lower scores because their writing style differed from the training examples, even when their ideas were equally strong. These cases helped me understand that consistent results are not necessarily fair results. Higher insurance costs and unfair grades can affect people’s opportunities, finances, and confidence.

The lab also taught me that trusting AI depends on the situation. I compared ChatGPT and Gemini across four health scenarios. Both recognized the emergency scenario and responded more urgently than the fictional app in our activity. However, a few good responses do not prove that a tool will always be reliable. I learned to ask whether I can check an answer, how serious a mistake would be, and whether the harm could be undone. This matters because a confident answer can encourage someone to rely on a system even when they cannot judge its accuracy.

Voice cloning showed me how the same technology can help people or hurt them. It can help someone communicate using their own voice, but it can also be used to impersonate a family member and ask for money. In my lab, I suggested requiring permission and a live recording of a random phrase before creating a clone. That would make misuse harder, although it would not prevent every scam.

As someone who builds apps and wants to create useful products, these examples made ethics feel like part of the design process. I need to think about who might be treated unfairly, when users need another way to check an answer, and how someone could misuse a feature. A tool being useful is only part of what makes it worth building.
